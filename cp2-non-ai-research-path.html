CP2 Non-AI Research Path

About This Path

In this version of CP2, you will identify your discourse community and discover its vocabulary by following the language used in real sources.

You are doing manually what the Guided AI path uses AI to help begin: starting broad, following the conversation, noticing patterns, and gradually learning what the community calls things.

Step 1: Revisit Your Direction

Start with your CP1:

  • topic;
  • artifact; and
  • current research question.

Before going farther, ask whether you still want to spend the semester with them.

If you have realized that your topic is not interesting enough, your artifact does not actually give you much to investigate, or another direction has become more compelling, change it now.

This is the last point in the Culminating Project where a major change is relatively easy.

If you change your topic or artifact, identify the new one at the beginning of your CP2 submission.

Step 2: Start Broad

You probably do not yet know the exact name of your discourse community or the vocabulary you are looking for.

That is normal.

Begin by searching broadly around:

  • your topic;
  • your artifact;
  • your current research question;
  • the type of artifact you are studying;
  • or the larger issue surrounding it.

At this stage, you are looking for where the conversation is happening.

Follow links.

Notice authors and organizations that keep appearing.

Notice the names of fields, professions, communities, practices, or schools of thought.

Notice words that seem to have a more specific meaning inside the conversation than they would in ordinary speech.

Your search should gradually become narrower as you learn more.

Step 3: Identify the Discourse Community

Eventually, you should be able to name a group that is genuinely connected to your inquiry.

A discourse community is not simply “people interested in my topic.”

It is a group whose members communicate about an area using shared knowledge, practices, conventions, and specialized language.

Then write one paragraph identifying the discourse community you have chosen and explaining how it connects to your topic, artifact, and developing research question.

Step 4: Follow the Community’s Language

Now look more closely at places where that community actually communicates.

Depending on the community, useful sources might include:

  • scholarly articles;
  • professional organizations;
  • disciplinary or industry publications;
  • glossaries;
  • reference works;
  • news or trade publications;
  • community websites;
  • specialized forums or fan resources;
  • organizational documents;
  • or other places where members of the community actually use the language.

As you read, watch for terms that:

  • appear repeatedly;
  • seem to carry a specialized meaning;
  • divide one concept into more precise categories;
  • identify something outsiders might not know how to name; or
  • give you a better way to think about your own project.

Step 5: Move Beyond the Obvious

Do not collect random vocabulary merely to reach 10–15 terms.

Ask:

Will knowing this term help me investigate my project more intelligently?

Imagine your topic involves how ballet dancers are treated by their companies or dance troupes.

You may quickly encounter distinctions between pointe shoes and flat shoes.

Those are real terms.

But if your developing question is about emotional pressure, workplace hierarchy, or treatment by directors, defining types of shoes may contribute almost nothing to the project.

If you are also investigating:

  • physical injury;
  • bodily discipline;
  • training expectations;
  • pain;
  • or how equipment affects dancers’ bodies,

then those same terms may suddenly become useful.

So the test is not:

Is this a real word used by the community?

That is only the beginning.

The better question is:

Does learning this word give me something useful for the inquiry I am building?

Your vocabulary should become more selective and more specialized as you search.

Step 6: Document Where You Found Each Term

For each of your 10–15 terms, include:

  1. The term
  2. Your definition in your own words
  3. The source where you found the term being used

Because you encountered the term in a real source rather than receiving it from AI, you do not need a separate verification source.

Finding it in authentic community discourse is already part of the evidence.

But you do need to identify where you found it.

Step 7: Define It Yourself

Do not paste someone else’s definition into the assignment.

Read how the term is being used.

Look elsewhere if you need more context.

Then explain it in language that shows you understand what it means.

Your definition does not need to sound like a dictionary.

It needs to be accurate.

Step 8: Update Your Research Question

End with the current version of your research question.

The vocabulary you learned should have given you a more precise understanding of your subject.

Your revised question may include some of your new terms if they genuinely help you ask what you mean.

It does not need to.

Do not decorate the question with jargon merely to demonstrate that you learned vocabulary.

Use the language that asks the strongest question.

What Good Work Looks Like

Strong CP2 work:

  • follows a broad search into a specific discourse community;
  • finds vocabulary in places where that community actually communicates;
  • moves beyond obvious or merely topical words;
  • selects terms because they are useful to the developing project;
  • defines those terms in the student’s own words;
  • documents where each term was found; and
  • ends with a research question that reflects what the student now understands.

The searching is part of the assignment.

You are learning not only what this community calls things, but also where its conversations happen and how to enter them.
